Security operations centres are asked to watch LLM agents that read untrusted content and act
through tools. We measure what a SIEM fed by the application's telemetry can see of attacks on
such agents, using an open pack of 25 Microsoft Sentinel rules (23 also for Wazuh), 112{,}894 items
from 14 public datasets, two open injection classifiers, and live attacks in one demo application on
twelve models: five small open-weight models, three Azure-hosted OpenAI models and four Claude models
behind the Anthropic API. Content signatures fire on under 1\% of benign prompts but recall only 12\% of
attack prompts, and lose almost everything to rewording. Against models that
comply, rules over what the agent did or produced caught all 269 successful attacks across our ten
scenarios. Against three Claude models (Haiku~4.5, Sonnet~5.5, Opus~5.5) those rules saw 13\% of 300 attack sessions,
and none on Opus~5.5, because the attacks failed. In a pre-registered confirmation, logging provider
refusals as events and matching refusal language in the answer raised this to 70\% (72\% on Opus,
almost all from Anthropic's serving-side classifier) at an 8\% benign rate, so it measures attempts
rather than acting as a precise detector. An instruction placed beyond the application's logged preview
was followed but did not appear in the log, and Azure's default content filter blocked nothing past
the first 1{,}000 characters of a user message and nothing in tool results. When the agent did not act on an injected
request to email an internal document to an outside address, nine of twelve models told the user to
do it, as the next step, in 61\% to 100\% of their runs (1{,}440 in all, labelled by language models); only Claude Sonnet~5.5 and the two Opus models warned. One of two cross-family raters
disagreed with the labels, so this generality claim missed its pre-registered criterion; the other
agreed on 97\% of items.
