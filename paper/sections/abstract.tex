Security operations centres are asked to watch LLM agents that read untrusted content and act
through tools. We measure what a SIEM fed by the application's own telemetry can see of attacks on
such agents, using an open pack of 25 Microsoft Sentinel rules (23 also for Wazuh), 112{,}894 items
from 14 public datasets, two open injection classifiers, and live attacks in one demo application on
twelve models: five small open-weight models, three Azure-hosted OpenAI models and four Claude models
behind the Anthropic API. Content signatures are precise and blind: they fire on under 1\% of benign
prompts, recall 12\% of attack prompts, and lose almost everything to rewording. Against models that
comply, rules over what the agent did or produced caught all 269 successful attacks across our ten
scenarios. Against three Claude models (Haiku~4.5, Sonnet~5.5, Opus~5.5) those rules saw 13\% of 300 attack sessions,
and none on Opus~5.5, because the attacks failed. In a pre-registered confirmation, logging provider
refusals as events and matching refusal language in the answer raised this to 70\% (72\% on Opus,
almost all from Anthropic's serving-side classifier) at an 8\% benign rate; this is coverage of
attempts, not a precise detector. We show that the model, the application log and a commercial
guardrail inspect different parts of the same input. Finally, when the agent did not act on an injected
request to email an internal document to an outside address, nine of twelve models told the user to
do it, as the next step, in 61\% to 100\% of their runs (1{,}440 runs in all, labelled by language
models); only Claude Sonnet~5.5 and the two Opus models warned. One of our two cross-family raters
disagreed with the labels, so this generality claim did not meet its pre-registered criterion; the
other agreed closely. Rules, agent, pre-registrations, raw runs and analysis code are public.
