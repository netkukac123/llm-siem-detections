\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{One application.} All live results come from one demo agent with one system prompt and one
tool set. Real assistants differ in prompts, tools, guardrails and in how much telemetry they emit;
the behavioural rules in particular assume fields (provenance, risk class, approval state) that many
applications do not log today.

\paragraph{Small open models.} The five models fit an 8~GB GPU. Frontier models refuse more and
follow injections differently; the harness is model-agnostic and the frontier comparison is the next
phase of this work.

\paragraph{Oracles.} Compliance and harm oracles capture the attacker goal of each scenario, not every
possible harm, and a model can comply in ways the oracle does not anticipate.

\paragraph{Dataset labels.} Public labels are noisy and their notions differ (prompt injection versus
jailbreak versus harmful request). Several jailbreak sets contain role-play prompts that no
injection signature is meant to catch; we report per dataset so that this is visible. Benign corpora
are not production traffic.

\paragraph{Author bias.} The rules and the live scenarios have the same author. RQ2 and RQ3 rely on
third-party attacks; RQ4 generates attacks automatically. Error analysis of samples was done by the
author with AI assistance and is reported qualitatively.
